The Latine American community has for decades faced economic disparities and racial injustices—burdens felt particularly by the working class, who are primarily employed in the agricultural sector^1^—often involving physically demanding labor and hazardous conditions. These hardships are compounded by racial discrimination and systemic oppression especially in the undocumented community. Massive educational barriers feed into this cycle, despite high educational expectations and hopes expressed by Latine families. Data from the US Census Bureau showed that 31% of Hispanic adults have not completed high school—over 2x any other ethnic group.^2^

All of these disparities are what social scientists call the social determinants of health—a term highlighting the strong inverse correlation between socio-economic and educational status and health outcomes. Indeed, when a family struggles to put food on the table, expenses like health insurance must often be first cut. With this framework, the Hispanic community is at especially high risk of poorer health outcomes…right?

Surprisingly, decades of national data reveal the opposite—that compared to people of other ethnic origins, Hispanic Americans have lower rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, stroke, and infant mortality.^3^ Hispanic Americans have an average life expectancy in the US of 82.2 years—which is more than three years higher than the non-Hispanic White population.^4^ Zooming in, social scientists have discovered an even more perplexing phenomenon—that among the Mexican American population, Mexican immigrants had much better health outcomes than American-born Mexican people. This is what social scientists term the Immigrant Paradox, which has been observed in other ethnic groups as well. Contrary to expectations, acculturated individuals seem to display poorer health outcomes than immigrants, even though immigrants often face more socioeconomic stressors.^5^

For decades, the Hispanic Paradox has puzzled many in the scientific community and has been met with valid skepticism. First, many social scientists caution against generalizing this paradox to all Hispanic American communities, as this is a community made up of many diverse cultures, situations, and origins that have differing health outcomes when analyzed individually. One explanation for the Hispanic Paradox may be the healthy migrant hypothesis—that only the healthiest people ventured to and were successful at migrating to the United States from Central and South America—which would make sense with the physically taxing journey and labor that Hispanic immigrants/migrants are often faced with. Others suggest that the underreporting of deaths of undocumented individuals, or the return of undocumented immigrants to their home country when health declines, may lead to biased data. However, research has shown that this does not completely account for the observed trends in the Hispanic paradox.^6^

Some point to cultural connection and collectivism as factors that empower Hispanic communities despite the hardships they face, possibly improving health outcomes. One major aspect of culture is food: compared to the “American” diet, which is high in processed foods and trans fats, traditional Latin American diets are rich in natural foods like beans, great sources of fiber and nutrition and known to lower rates of cardiovascular disease. Further, collectivist values are vital to many Latin cultures and lead to a greater sense of cohesiveness and mutual support, which may contribute to better mental and physical health. In contrast, the “American” culture adopted more by acculturated individuals holds a greater emphasis on individualism, leading to loneliness and isolation that feed into mental health struggles. These theories emphasize the often-overlooked role of culture and community in mental and physical well being and longevity.

Another answer can also be found in a surprising place—epigenetics, the study of factors influencing gene expression, such as DNA methylation. In 2016, UCLA conducted a large study^7^ that compared patterns of DNA methylation among samples of blood from individuals of different ethnic origins. In their analysis, the researchers used what they knew about methylation patterns at different ages to predict the epigenetic, or biological, age of the samples, and how much that deviated from the actual age of the subject. Intriguingly, according to these epigenetic “time clocks,” they discovered that Latine blood aged more slowly than non-Latine blood. In fact, the epigenetic age of Latine women after menopause was, on average, 2.4 years younger than their non-Latino counterparts. Of the Latin American population, the Indigenous Tsimane people of Bolivia had the “youngest” epigenetic ages.

What is the explanation behind the Hispanic Paradox? The answer probably lies in a complex confluence of many factors, including culture, epigenetics, under-reporting in data, and may vary widely between specific ethnic groups. However, it suggests that perhaps we ought to rethink our understanding of the social determinants of health, expanding our paradigm to include the protective roles of culture, community, and even epigenetics. Indeed, current evidence suggests that communities are not powerless in the face of injustice and hardship—that they remain empowered by mysterious mechanisms of intrinsic resilience.
